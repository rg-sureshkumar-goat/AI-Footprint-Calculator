Other findings:
- **Settings change the result a lot.**
  - The choice of model alone can change energy up to 46× (Bertazzini).
  - Doubling resolution raised energy 1.3× to 4.7×, depending on the model.
  - Doubling steps roughly doubled energy (MIT TR).
  - Prompt length had no statistically significant effect (Bertazzini).
- **Higher quality doesn't always cost more.** Some of the highest-quality models were also among the most efficient (Bertazzini).
- **For comparison,** the calculator's current EcoLogits figure for one GPT-5.5 chatbot reply is ~2.7 Wh ([data.js](assets/js/data.js)). So one image falls roughly in the same range as a text reply, from well below it to a few times above it, depending on the model.

### Limitations and contrary points
- **None of these sources measured the commercial tools creative staff are likely to use**, such as Midjourney, OpenAI's image models, Google's or Adobe Firefly. I found no per-image energy disclosure from any of them. Bertazzini left DALL·E and Midjourney out because they aren't publicly accessible, and MIT TR describes the closed providers as a "black box." That is a search result, not proof that no disclosure exists.
- **The hardware differs across studies:** data-center A100 and H100 GPUs versus a consumer RTX 4090. Measurement scope differs too (GPU only vs. whole system), so the numbers aren't directly comparable.
- **MIT TR's doubling for overhead is a rule of thumb** that its own methodology says is untested for diffusion models. It's also unclear whether the ~1.2 Wh figure at 50 steps is GPU-only or already doubled.
- **The models measured are older open-source ones.** Newer commercial models may use different architectures, so figures could be higher or lower.
- **Bertazzini is a preprint.** I haven't found a peer-reviewed version.
- **EcoLogits, the calculator's current data source, covers text and video but not images** ([ecologits.ai](https://ecologits.ai/latest/)). Image figures would need a new source.

### Assumptions still to check
- **Water per image:** I found no direct figure. Any water number would have to be derived from energy × data-center and grid water intensity, which is an assumption to state openly.
- **How many images a professional generates per task or day,** including discarded variations. I have no evidence on this yet, and it may dominate the total more than the per-image figure does.
- **Which tools the company's staff actually use.** That's for you or the client to confirm.

| EcoLogits video method, June 2026 ([method](https://ecologits.ai/latest/methodology/video_generation/), [announcement](https://ecologits.ai/latest/blog/2026/06/18/video-impacts/)) | Built on Jegham et al.: latency regressions × whole-server power × data-center overhead (PUE) | Gives energy, carbon, **water** and hardware-manufacturing ranges per video for commercial providers including Google, OpenAI, Runway, Kling and ByteDance |

**What drives the cost:**
- Energy rises roughly with the *square* of resolution and of video length, and in direct proportion to the number of denoising steps (Delavande).
- So doubling the length or the resolution costs much more than double.

**For scale,** using the calculator's figure of ~2.7 Wh for one GPT-5.5 chatbot reply:
- One Veo 3 clip ≈ about 10 replies.
- One Sora 2 Pro 1080p clip ≈ several hundred replies.
- Unlike images, video can be far above text.

### Limitations and contrary points
- **The commercial-model figures are estimates, not measurements.** Jegham et al. inferred them from API timings and *assumed* hardware, for example that Veo runs on Google TPU v6e chips. The providers haven't confirmed them.
- **The sources disagree for the same model family.** CogVideoX-5B is ~25 Wh in Delavande, but MIT TR reports ~940 Wh for a newer CogVideoX. That is probably due to different versions and settings, but I can't fully reconcile the two.
- **EcoLogits assigns the whole server's power to each request,** with no sharing across simultaneous requests. That likely pushes estimates up.
- **Two of the key sources are preprints** (Jegham, Delavande), and all involve the same researcher (Luccioni). They are not independent confirmations of each other.
- **Funding:** EcoLogits' video work came from the "GenAI footprint Alliance," a Publicis Groupe initiative whose members include AXA and Engie. That doesn't make it wrong, but it's worth telling readers who worry about corporate motives.
- **Audio isn't well covered.** Delavande didn't study it, and Jegham calls their audio estimates less grounded.

### Assumptions still to check
- **How many clips a professional generates per finished shot.** Jegham et al. cite reports that one AI-generated Coca-Cola ad involved over 70,000 generated videos. That is secondhand from a news report, one extreme case, and not yet verified by me. If iteration dominates the total, a calculator should probably ask about attempts, not just final outputs.
- **Water per video:** EcoLogits' figure is *derived* (energy × water intensity), not measured.
- **Which video tools the company uses,** and at what length and resolution.
- **Whether EcoLogits' calculator output matches the paper's figures.** I haven't run it yet. That's a check for the specification stage.

I'd propose all four sources as **"use with qualifications,"** with Jegham/EcoLogits as the main data path because they cover commercial tools. That's your decision.

## Draft: Coding agents and long, irregular sessions (serves Jordan)

### Key finding: the current "agent session" is measured differently from how real sessions work
- **The calculator's current method.** It treats one agent session as **100,000 output tokens** and applies EcoLogits' per-output-token energy. That gives, for example, **~459 Wh** (range 314–604) for Claude Opus 4.8 and **~644 Wh** for GPT-5.5 ([data.js](assets/js/data.js)).
- **EcoLogits counts only output tokens.** Its LLM method estimates energy as *number of output tokens × energy per output token* and does not model input tokens ([EcoLogits LLM method](https://ecologits.ai/latest/methodology/llm_inference/)).
- **Real agent sessions are dominated by input tokens.** Across 8 frontier models on 500 SWE-bench Verified coding tasks, agentic tasks used **~1,000× more tokens** than code chat, and **input tokens, not output, drove the cost**, even with caching (Bai et al. 2026, below).
- So the current figure could be too high or too low for a given person, because it counts the wrong kind of token. I can't say which direction without better per-token evidence.

### Evidence (checked directly)

| Source | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| Bai, Huang, Wang, Sun, Mihalcea, Brynjolfsson, Pentland & Pei, "How Do AI Agents Spend Your Money?", arXiv, Apr 2026 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22750)) | ~1,000× more tokens than chat, driven by input tokens. **Runs of the same task varied up to 30× in total tokens.** Some models used over 1.5 million more tokens than others on the same tasks. More tokens did not mean more accuracy. Models underestimated their own token needs. |
| Couch, "Electricity use of AI coding agents," personal blog, 20 Jan 2026 ([post](https://simonpcouch.com/blog/2026-01-20-cc-impact/)) | From his own Claude Code logs (8,825 API calls): the **median session had 24 model calls and ~592,000 tokens**, mostly cache reads. He estimated **~41 Wh per median session** and **~1,300 Wh on a median working day** running 2–3 agents at once, which he likens to an extra dishwasher run per day. |

For scale: Couch's 41 Wh session ≈ 15 GPT-5.5 chatbot replies at the calculator's figure, while his 1,300 Wh day ≈ ~500.

### Limitations and contrary points
- **Couch calls his own figures "napkin math from Some Guy."** They are one person's usage on one model (Opus 4.5). His energy per token is derived from Epoch AI's GPT-4o estimate and scaled by *API prices*, assuming energy tracks price. That's a large, unverified assumption, and he says "I have no idea if these are reasonable guesses" about the cached-token figures. He deliberately used pessimistic long-context rates. *Proposed decision: use only as an illustration of session structure, not as a figure.*
- **Bai et al. is a preprint** that measures *tokens and dollars, not energy*. SWE-bench tasks are benchmark bug fixes, which may not resemble Jordan's creative-technology sessions.
- **No source I found measures energy per cached input token.** Caching reuses earlier computation, so it is presumably cheaper, but how much cheaper is not published. This is the biggest gap.
- **None of the providers publish per-session energy for their coding agents.**

### What this means for Jordan's stated needs
- **"Prompts per day is too simple":** supported. One user message sets off many model calls (Couch's median session was 24 calls for 5 user messages), and the same task can vary 30×.
- **Wants assumptions, ranges and project totals:** the evidence supports wide ranges, and an input for session count or length rather than one fixed session size. Any per-session figure would have to be shown as highly uncertain.

### Assumptions still to check

- How long and how frequent Jordan-like sessions are in practice (no evidence yet beyond one blogger).
- Whether Anthropic's own documentation gives average daily usage for its coding tool that could serve as a second data point. Not checked yet.

Quick update: I've finished checking the evidence on per-token energy. The gap can be narrowed but not closed. Findings below.

## Per-input-token energy: what the evidence can and can't support

### Bottom line
- **No public source measures energy per input token, or per cached input token, for the frontier cloud models people actually use for coding.**
- Three *estimation* methods exist, and for **fresh input tokens they roughly agree to within a few times**.
- For **cached input tokens**, which make up most of an agent session, **there is no evidence-based figure at all.** Only a price-based guess exists.

### What each method implies for ~10,000 extra input tokens

| Source | Method | Implied energy for 10k input tokens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Josh You, "How much energy does ChatGPT use?", Epoch AI, 2025 ([article](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/how-much-energy-does-chatgpt-use)) | Compute model for GPT-4o from assumed parameter count, hardware and utilisation. Self-described as "somewhat pessimistic." | ~**2.1 Wh**: a 10k-input query is ~2.4 Wh vs ~0.3 Wh for a typical one. A 100k-input query is ~40 Wh, because cost grows roughly with the square of input length. |
| Jegham, Abdelatti, Koh, Elmoubarki & Hendawi, "How Hungry is AI?", arXiv 2025, v6 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09598)) | Inferred from public API speed data plus assumed hardware; not directly metered | **Under ~1.7 Wh**. GPT-4.1 goes from 3.16 Wh (1k input, 1k output) to 4.83 Wh (10k input, 1.5k output), and that increase also includes 500 more output tokens. |
| Couch 2026 (already noted) | API prices used as a stand-in for energy | ~**2 to 4 Wh**, depending on which context-length rate is used |

**Cached tokens:**
- Couch assumes cached reads cost 1/10 the energy of fresh input, only because Anthropic *charges* 1/10. He says he has "no idea" whether that's reasonable.
- A search result claimed "cache hits use 90% less energy." It traced to a vendor blog with no measurement behind it. **I propose rejecting it.**
- The only peer-reviewed-style evidence is qualitative. Caching avoids recomputation and saves operating energy, but the storage hardware adds manufacturing carbon ([Tian et al. 2025, "GreenCache," arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23970)).

### Why the measured lab studies don't fill the gap
- **The strongest measurement studies use small open models.** ML.Energy (Chung et al. 2026, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22076)) and Vellaisamy et al. (IISWC 2026, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28044)) both do.
- **Vellaisamy et al. show that "energy per token" is itself a slippery unit.** Longer outputs lowered joules per token while *raising* total energy (7.46 → 0.72 J/token, but 1.19 → 5.93 kJ total). One fixed per-token rate hides that.
- **Studies that measure energy for whole agent tasks run small local models on consumer or workstation GPUs** (for example SWEnergy, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.09543); AgentStop, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.15206)). I read only the titles and abstracts, and they don't represent cloud frontier models.

### What this means for the calculator (options for you, not decisions)
1. **Keep the current output-only EcoLogits figure** and clearly disclose that it ignores input tokens and doesn't reflect how real sessions are structured.
2. **Add an input-token component** using the ~1–4 Wh per 10k fresh tokens range, and show cached tokens as an explicitly unknown range rather than a single figure.
3. **Show a session-level range** built from the structure evidence (Bai et al. on variability, Couch as a worked example), clearly labelled as an illustration.

Any of these would meet Jordan's stated need for visible assumptions and ranges. Options 2 and 3 introduce more uncertainty that has to be communicated.

**Proposed source decisions:**
- **Use with qualifications:** Epoch AI and Jegham 2025.
- **Background only, for mechanism:** Chung, Vellaisamy and GreenCache.
- **Reject:** the "90% less energy" cache claim.

### Criticism and contrary evidence
- **Experts disputed Google's figures on two points,** as reported by The Verge. I verified this through [PCWorld's coverage](https://www.pcworld.com/article/2886730/experts-are-skeptical-about-googles-ai-water-consumption-claims.html), 22 Aug 2025, because the Verge article itself didn't load.

- **Google's paper hadn't been peer reviewed** when published.
- **In fairness to Google,** the paper argues a *median* is more representative than a mean, because a small share of heavy prompts skews the average.

### Why the numbers disagree (for Robin's explanation)
1. **Which prompt:** Google's median mixes many models, including small ones. The calculator shows specific flagship models at a fixed reply length.
2. **What's measured:** Google's figure is measured in production. EcoLogits, Epoch and Jegham model or infer.
3. **What's counted for water:** on-site cooling only (0.26 mL), on-site plus power plants (EcoLogits ~1–22 mL for a 300-word reply), or the full life cycle including manufacturing (Mistral, 45 mL).
4. **How carbon is counted:** market-based (lowered by clean-energy purchases) vs. location-based (the actual grid).
5. **When:** figures change fast. Google reports a 33× drop in one year, so 2025 figures may not describe 2026 models.

## Draft: Water (serves Alex; relevant to all)

### Evidence (checked directly)

**1. What counts as "AI's water"** (Li, Yang, Islam & Ren, "Making AI Less 'Thirsty'," *Communications of the ACM* 68(7):54–61, 2025, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3724499); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03271)):
- **Scope 1:** on-site water evaporated to cool the data center.
- **Scope 2:** off-site water used by power plants to generate its electricity.
- **Scope 3:** water used in manufacturing the chips and servers.
- **Withdrawal vs. consumption:**
  - *Withdrawal* is water taken from a source, much of it returned.
  - *Consumption* is water evaporated or otherwise removed, which affects downstream supply.
  - AI figures are usually *consumption*.

**2. In the US, power-plant water far outweighs cooling water:**
- Shehabi et al., *2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report*, Berkeley Lab, Dec 2024 ([PDF](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2024-12/lbnl-2024-united-states-data-center-energy-usage-report_1.pdf)):
  - US data centers **directly consumed ~66 billion liters in 2023** (84% at hyperscale and colocation sites).
  - The **indirect footprint through electricity was ~800 billion liters,** about **12× the direct figure.**
  - Indirect intensity: **4.52 L/kWh** for data centers vs. **4.35 L/kWh** US average.
  - Average on-site water use per kWh was **~0.36 L/kWh** in 2023.
- Guidi & Dominici, "The Hidden Water Geography of U.S. Hyperscale Data Centers in the AI Era," arXiv, June 2026 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02531)):
  - Across 472 hyperscale sites, **electricity-related water is about three-quarters** of the ~300 GL per year operational total (range 205–451).

- Guidi & Dominici: **cooling burdens cluster in water-stressed western and south-central basins,** while **electricity-related burdens cluster in a few eastern, fossil-heavy grid regions.** Just 3 of 24 grid operators account for 59% of electricity-related water.

## Draft: Trust and communication (serves Robin)

### Evidence (checked directly)

**1. Showing uncertainty as numbers or ranges doesn't meaningfully damage trust.**
- **van der Bles, van der Linden, Freeman & Spiegelhalter, *PNAS* 117(14):7672–7683, 2020** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1913678117)).
  - 5 experiments, including a preregistered national replication and a field experiment on BBC News (n = 5,780).
  - Communicating uncertainty produced "only a small decrease in trust in numbers and trustworthiness of the source, and **mostly for verbal uncertainty**."
- **Gustafson & Rice, *Public Understanding of Science* 29(6):614–633, 2020** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963662520942122)).
  - A review of 48 experiments. **Quantified error ranges and probabilities had "only positive or null effects."**
  - Negative effects came mostly from **uncertainty framed as scientists disagreeing** ("consensus uncertainty"). That framing never had positive effects.
  - Prior beliefs and worldviews moderated the effects.
- **Dries, Schneider, Oeberst, McDowell, Egharevba & Rebitschek, *Royal Society Open Science* 13(4), 2026** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.251896)).
  - A meta-analysis of 28 studies. Uncertainty communication "neither systematically undermined nor enhanced trust" in the source.
  - It notes most studies used fictitious contexts, not real communicators.

**2. Vague words for uncertainty are widely misread.**
- **Budescu, Broomell & Por, *Psychological Science* 20(3):299–308, 2009** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02284.x)).
- When people put numbers on the IPCC's words ("likely," "very likely"), their answers deviated significantly from the IPCC's own definitions, **even when they had the definitions in front of them.**

**3. Disclosing a conflict of interest doesn't automatically fix trust.**
- **Cain, Loewenstein & Moore, "The Dirt on Coming Clean," *Journal of Legal Studies* 34(1):1–25, 2005** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/426699); abstract via [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jlstud/v34y2005p1-25.html)).
- People "do not discount advice from biased advisors as much as they should" even after disclosure, and **disclosure can make advisors exaggerate more.**

**4. Employees distrust sustainability messaging they see as greenwashing.**
- **Mu & Lee, *Sustainability* 15(22):15693, 2023** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/su152215693)): among 304 employees, perceived greenwashing was **negatively related to trust** and, through trust, to loyalty.
- **Ma et al., *Business Ethics, the Environment & Responsibility* 34(4):1475–1486, 2024** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/beer.12723)): among 232 employees, perceived greenwashing was **negatively related to employees' own green behavior.**

### What this suggests for Robin's stated needs (for your judgement)
- **Show ranges as numbers, not vague words.** This is the best-supported finding. Ranges carry little trust cost, while vague words are both less trusted and misread. This fits the earlier sections, where most figures are genuinely ranges.
- **Present disagreement carefully.** Sources that really do conflict (Google vs. its critics, TikTok vs. Greenly, the CogVideoX figures) are "consensus uncertainty," the kind most linked to lost trust. The evidence doesn't say to hide these disagreements. The brief requires honesty. But explaining *why* sources differ (different boundaries and methods), as in sections 4 and 10, may be better than simply showing competing numbers.
- **Disclose funding, but don't treat it as enough on its own.** Examples include Netflix funding the Carbon Trust study (S25), Publicis funding EcoLogits' video work (S6), and the company's own role. Disclosure is ethically required, but Cain et al. suggest it doesn't by itself make an audience trust appropriately. Visible sources and methods probably matter more.
- **An employer-provided tool starts with a credibility risk.** If employees see it as promotional, trust and engagement fall. That supports the brief's rule that the calculator must argue neither that AI is harmless nor that it is harmful.

### Limitations
- **Most uncertainty experiments use fictitious scenarios and general audiences,** not employees using a company's own tool (Dries et al.).
- **The greenwashing studies are surveys of employees in China,** measuring associations, not causes. They may not transfer to a US creative-media company.
- **Cain et al. study advisors paid to bias estimates,** which is a different situation from a company publishing a calculator.
- **No study was found on employer-provided environmental calculators specifically.**
- **Robin is a fictional profile.** These findings describe tendencies, not what any particular skeptical employee will think.

- **Cain et al. 2005:** use with qualifications.
