What your chatbot use actually costs

Log your AI use as it happens and this tool adds it up into one carbon and water cost, set against your own footprint, your team’s, and your company’s. Per-model figures come from EcoLogits, an open-source estimator of the energy, carbon, and water behind each AI model.

What’s counted, and how reliable it is

Carbon uses the average grid intensity of the region you pick, not a single whole-world average, plus the hardware’s embodied emissions. Training, image and video generation, and retries are excluded — training is too uncertain to attribute, though split across all users it adds little to your personal footprint. The Claude numbers are the shakiest here: Anthropic hasn’t said how Claude is built, so EcoLogits has to guess, and its guess might run low. Everything else is sourced in the methodology.

How these numbers are made

Per-model AI estimates. The energy, carbon, and water numbers for each prompt come from the open-source EcoLogits library (v0.10), the same code that runs the EcoLogits calculator. The model list is current as of June 2026. EcoLogits estimates electricity from how big each model is (really, how many parameters it uses on each token) and how long the reply is, then adds the cost of manufacturing the hardware. The low and high ends are EcoLogits' own 95% confidence interval, which mostly comes down to nobody outside these companies knowing how big the closed models really are. One of its guesses matters a lot here: Anthropic has never said how Claude is built, and EcoLogits' model data assumes Claude Opus and Sonnet are mixture-of-experts models, which would mean they only use a fraction of their parameters on any given token (it marks every Claude entry "model-arch-not-released"). People who track this disagree. Some think the clues we do have, like Claude's speed and pricing, point to dense models that use every parameter every time. Others think Claude is a very large mixture-of-experts. If the dense camp is right, the Claude numbers on this page are probably too low. The water numbers count consumption, not withdrawal: water that is actually gone, evaporated cooling the data center or generating its electricity, not water taken in and then returned. Every water figure on this page works this way, so the comparisons are apples to apples.

Carbon basis. EcoLogits gives each prompt's electricity use and its embodied (hardware-manufacturing) emissions separately. We keep EcoLogits' embodied figure as-is and cost the electricity on the grid where you live, using carbon intensities from Ember 2024 and Our World in Data: roughly 380 (US), 215 (EU), 125 (UK), 580 (China), 700 (India), and 480 (world) g CO2e/kWh. Water is left on EcoLogits' global basis.

Output and words. Each row maps to an EcoLogits output-token count: tweet (50), short email (170), article summary (250), chatbot reply (400), 5-page report (5,000), long document (15,000), and re-writing the Lord of the Rings trilogy (500,000), an EcoLogits benchmark task; the trilogy itself runs about 480,000 words. Cost scales with length, so long generations dominate. Counts are per day; annual figures multiply by 365. Words assume OpenAI's ~0.75 words per token, read at 238 words a minute (Brysbaert 2019). An earlier version offered a "coding / agent session" row of 100,000 output tokens. It was removed because real agent sessions are mostly input, not output (see below); old log entries and share-link rows with that size are now ignored.

Agent sessions and projects. A coding-agent session reads far more than it writes: agentic coding uses about 1,000× more tokens than chat, driven by input, and runs of the same task can differ by up to 30× in tokens (Bai et al. 2026, a preprint). Most of that input is cached context read again on each model call. So sessions are logged in the tracker by tokens of each type, as tools such as Claude Code report them, and costed in three parts, each with a central value and a range: output tokens × the chosen model's EcoLogits energy per output token (its 100,000-token "assist application development" figure ÷ 100,000, with its range); fresh-input and cache-write tokens × about 2.1 Wh per 10,000 (range 1–4); and cache-read tokens × about 0.39 Wh per 10,000 (range 0–4). EcoLogits does not model input tokens at all. The 2.1 Wh central value comes from Epoch AI (2025), which estimates a GPT-4o query with a 10,000-token input at about 2.4 Wh against about 0.3 Wh for a typical query; the 1–4 range spans that, Jegham et al. (2025) (a preprint inferring energy from API speeds), and the 3.9 Wh per 10,000 used by Couch (2026). Cache writes are new context processed for the first time and then stored, so they share the fresh-input rate. The cache-read central value, 0.39 Wh, is Couch's, derived by assuming energy tracks API prices, which Couch calls napkin math. Its 0–4 bounds are this calculator's own reasoning, not a measurement: a cache read avoids reprocessing, so it should cost no more than fresh input, and it is not free, because the stored context is still used during generation and storage has a hardware footprint. Because the two central values come from different sources, the cache-read rate is about one-fifth of the fresh-input rate, not the one-tenth that Couch's own pricing ratio implies. The input rates are GPT-4o-era, the same for every model (no source gives them per model), and flat, although input cost grows faster than the number of tokens as the context lengthens (Epoch AI). For realistic sessions the cache-read part is usually the largest and least certain. Carbon is all of the session's electricity on your region's grid, plus EcoLogits' embodied hardware carbon for the output tokens only; no source gives embodied carbon for input. Water is EcoLogits' own figure for the output, plus the input side's energy × 4.88 L/kWh from Berkeley Lab (derived, as for images), so the two parts use different water intensities. No public source measures input or cache energy for frontier cloud models, and no provider publishes energy per session. A session can carry a project name; the project view adds up a project's sessions, each of which already counts once in its own day's AI total. The project estimate applies the same method to typed-in totals, pre-filled with one developer's 28 days on one repository (Peng, Lin & Lee 2026, a preprint; sessions often ran unattended around the clock), and is never added to the AI total. That example and Couch's reference session are single developers, not typical use. Sessions count toward your AI total only while “Use my log” is on.

Your digital day. A separate total for video calls, video streaming, music, social media, and gaming, shown beside the AI total and never as a share of it; the session tracker, budget, and team view never read it. Each activity's energy is hours × device power, plus hours × data per hour × network intensity, plus data-centre energy where a figure exists, following the full breakdown in the Carbon Trust's 2021 white paper on video streaming (European averages for 2020, seed-funded by Netflix through DIMPACT). Device power: smartphone 1 W, laptop 22 W, office desktop with monitor 115 W, and TV 100 W (“conservative for 2020”), each a single estimate with no range. Console: 216 W gaming (213–219) and 45 W on the home menu (43–47), used as a stand-in for streaming, from Sony's PS5 measurements; the central values are our midpoints. Gaming PC: about 373 W, the 358.6 W measured for one game by Pérez et al. (2024) plus a 14 W monitor (the ENERGY STAR median), with a range of 315–388 W from the 305.1 W Steam average modelled by Hazas et al. (2026) plus a 10 W monitor, to 358.6 W plus 29 W. The home router is counted at 10 W for each hour of home Wi-Fi use, and not on cellular. When activities overlap, the typical day's Wi-Fi hours can be lowered, and logged activities are treated as ending when they were logged, so overlapping logs count the router once; the two parts are added, capped at 24 hours. The Carbon Trust's headline 71 Wh an hour for routers is not used, because it divides router energy by data volume. The network is costed at 0.0065 kWh/GB on fixed broadband and 0.1 kWh/GB on mobile, although Mytton, Lundén & Malmodin (2024) find network energy is not proportional to data volume, and the Carbon Trust's own power model gives a much smaller, flatter network figure. Data per hour: streaming 1, 3, or 7 GB for SD, HD, or 4K on broadband and 0.17, 0.25, or 3 GB on mobile data settings (Carbon Trust, from Netflix); calls 0.5–3.4 GB by app, from CableLabs (2021), with a central 1.95 GB that is our own midpoint, since CableLabs does not say which app used how much; music about 11–144 MB from Spotify's quality settings. Social media and gaming have no network share, because no verified data-use figure was found. Data centres: about 1 Wh per hour of video streaming (Carbon Trust) and a flat 15 Wh a day for any social media use, from Meta's 2024 electricity of 0.0055 MWh per daily active person, a company-wide yearly average across all workloads; none for calls, music, or gaming. An activity logged in the tracker today replaces that activity's typical-day hours, so the total can mix a record with an estimate, and the panel shows which is which. Carbon is energy × your region's grid intensity. Water is device, router, and network electricity × 4.35 L/kWh and data centres × 4.88 L/kWh, US averages from Berkeley Lab, so it does not change with the region. Device manufacturing is not included here. The AI total does not count your own device or router, so the two totals use different boundaries.

Generated images. Images are logged in the session tracker, one per image made, including variations you discard. Every image uses one general range, with no choice of tool or settings, because no commercial image provider (such as DALL·E or Midjourney) publishes per-image figures. The central value, 1.35 Wh, is the median across the image models in Luccioni, Jernite & Strubell (2024), who measured 2022–23 open models on one A100 GPU (1.35 kWh per 1,000 images); the high end, 11.49 Wh, is their least efficient model (Stable Diffusion XL base). The low end, 0.086 Wh, is the lowest of 17 open models run on a consumer RTX 4090 by Bertazzini et al. (2025), a preprint. Each model ran at its own default resolution and steps, so the range already mixes settings; there are no settings inputs because doubling the resolution raised energy 1.3–4.7× (Bertazzini et al.), doubling the diffusion steps roughly doubled it (MIT Technology Review, 2025), commercial tools rarely expose steps, and no study measured above 1024 pixels. The studies also drew different system boundaries (a cloud A100 including idle power from the machine's other GPUs, a whole workstation, or a GPU with an assumed doubling for overhead), so the figures are not strictly comparable. Carbon is the energy on your region's grid, with no embodied hardware carbon, since no image study reports one. Water is derived: energy × 4.88 L/kWh, which is the US data-centre average on-site water use (0.36 L/kWh) plus the water used to generate their electricity (4.52 L/kWh) in 2023, from Berkeley Lab's 2024 US data center report. That matches the scope of the EcoLogits text figures (cooling plus power-plant water), but it is modelled, US-only, and, as its authors state, ignores renewable-power contracts; the location of the data centre that made your image is unknown. Images are counted in your AI total only while “Use my log” is on.

Your footprint. "Where you live" is a regional baseline for goods, services, and shared infrastructure (per-capita consumption from Our World in Data); home energy and driving are separate so they do not double-count. Home runs from a small apartment to a big house, about 1.5 to 7 t CO2e a year (EIA RECS; Goldstein et al. 2020). Driving uses EPA's ~400 g CO2/mile across roughly 3,000 to 25,000 miles a year (FHWA). Diet uses food footprints from Poore & Nemecek (2018) and Scarborough et al. (2023): about 1.05 t CO2e a year for a vegan diet up to 3.2 t for a heavy-meat one. Flying is anchored to a transatlantic round trip of about 1.6 t (Wynes & Nicholas 2017); the rarely / sometimes / often options are roughly 0.5, 1.5, and 5 such trips a year. The water footprint uses blue water only — freshwater actually drawn from rivers, lakes, and aquifers. We deliberately leave out green water (rain that falls on farmland and would evaporate anyway, whether or not a crop is grown) and grey water (a notional pollution-dilution volume), because neither is a real draw on freshwater supplies. The AI figure is already blue (data-center cooling and power-plant water), so both sides match. The per-person blue footprint is from the Water Footprint Network (Mekonnen & Hoekstra): about 450 m3/yr for the US and 153 m3 globally (the other regions are scaled by their irrigation intensity and are approximate). Removing green water changes the picture a lot: most of a food's water is rain, so a beef burger drops from ~1,700 L total to about 6 L of freshwater, while irrigated crops stay high.

Lifestyle cuts (the yearly chart). The "saved" figures come from the Founders Pledge Climate & Lifestyle report, drawing on Wynes & Nicholas (2017) (living car-free 2.4 t, avoiding a transatlantic flight 1.6 t, hang-drying, recycling, LED bulbs) and Ivanova et al. (2020) (green electricity, heat-pump heating, a home retrofit, electric and hybrid cars).

Ways you add emissions (the yearly chart). Manufacturing a new car ≈ 6 t CO2e (Berners-Lee, How Bad Are Bananas?); a new flat-screen TV ≈ 350 kg; a new laptop ≈ 250 kg; a new bicycle ≈ 100 kg and a new sofa ≈ 90 kg. Flights, per passenger (myclimate, with a transatlantic round trip ≈ 1.6 t per Wynes & Nicholas): a short-haul round trip ≈ 250 kg, a round-trip US cross-country flight ≈ 1 t, and a transatlantic round trip ≈ 1.6 t. A year of average US driving (~12,000 mi) ≈ 4.8 t at ~0.40 kg/mile (EPA). A beef burger every week for a year ≈ 156 kg (Poore & Nemecek, ~3 kg each); a year of daily coffee ≈ 77 kg; a cotton T-shirt ≈ 7 kg; a new smartphone ≈ 70 kg (Apple); and a pair of jeans ≈ 33 kg (Levi's).

Everyday things. A cup of coffee: ~0.21 kg CO2e and 140 L of water. An hour on a PS5 (~200 W); 3 minutes in a 1,200 W microwave; a mile in a gas car (EPA, ~0.40 kg); a 10-minute hot shower (~21 gal, EPA WaterSense, plus water heating); a dishwasher load (≤3.5 gal, ENERGY STAR); a dryer load (~3 kWh); printing a 400-page book (~2.7 kg, Wells 2012) with paper's water footprint; a beef burger (~3 kg, Poore & Nemecek; ~1,700 L at 15,400 L/kg beef); a new smartphone (~70 kg, Apple) needing ~12,800 L; a pair of jeans (~33 kg, Levi's) at ~10,000 L (Water Footprint Network).

How the appliance figures are derived. The coffee, PS5, microwave, shower, dishwasher, and dryer carbon numbers are each that item's measured electricity (cited above) times the US grid (~0.38 kg CO2/kWh), so they fall on cleaner grids. Two depend heavily on assumptions: a cup of coffee is ~0.05 kg black up to ~0.5 kg for a large latte (we use ~0.2 kg), and a 10-minute hot shower is roughly 0.2 kg on a gas water heater up to 1.5 kg on electric resistance (we use ~0.7 kg). Where a single authority does not exist, figures are rounded mid-range estimates from the sources cited, not false precision.

Water comparisons (blue water). The water charts use the blue (freshwater) component only, so the comparison items are the irrigation- and process-water heavy ones, from the Water Footprint Network and crop studies: a beef burger (~6 L, since beef is ~90% rainwater), a cup of coffee (~4 L), a slice of bread (~8 L), an egg (~15 L), a glass of milk (~22 L), a bowl of rice (~25 L), an avocado (~60 L), a handful of almonds (~120 L, as each California almond takes ~12 L of irrigation), a cotton T-shirt (~1,500 L of its 2,700 L total is blue), a pair of jeans (~5,000 L), and a new smartphone (mostly ultrapure freshwater). The savings come from cutting irrigated products (a few fewer cotton clothes) and outdoor watering: a typical lawn drinks roughly 20,000 gal of freshwater a year, so letting it go unwatered or replacing it with native plants saves most of that (EPA WaterSense). Eating less meat barely moves the blue total, because almost all of meat's water is rain. Energy and manufactured goods have a blue-water cost too: thermoelectric power plants evaporate about 0.47 gal of freshwater per kWh (NREL, cooling/consumption only), so an hour of central air conditioning (~3 kWh) consumes roughly 1.4 gal and a day of the average US home's electricity (~29 kWh) about 14 gal — the very same power-plant cooling water that makes up much of AI's footprint. Printing a 300-page book uses on the order of ~5 gal of process freshwater (paper's water footprint is mostly rain on trees, with the blue mill water lower since most is returned to rivers).
